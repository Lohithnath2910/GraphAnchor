Profile: Dr. Priya Raghunathan

Dr. Priya Raghunathan is a plant geneticist and the founding leader of the Seedvault North breeding project at the Marrow Institute of Applied Science. Since 2017 she has led the program that produced Brindlewheat-3, a drought-tolerant wheat variety now grown by about a third of the Calder Reach's wheat farmers, and she directs the conservation seed bank that holds the region's agricultural heritage.

Early life and education

Raghunathan grew up in a farming town in a dry region far from the Calder Reach, where her grandfather grew millet and her father taught mathematics. She remembers the summer when the monsoon failed, and her grandfather walked the fields in silence, rubbing a handful of dry soil between his fingers. She says that the memory is the reason she studies plants, and that every drought-tolerant line she has bred owes something to him.

She studied biology at a university in her home country and then won a scholarship to the Marrow Institute of Applied Science in the Calder Reach, where she began a doctorate in 2011. Her supervisor was Professor Ottoline Brandvik, who had directed the Institute since 2013 and who, before that, led the coastal engineering group. Raghunathan has said that it was an unusual match: Brandvik is an engineer and a modeler, while she was a geneticist, but Brandvik's encouragement to think about the plant as a system that responds to its environment shaped her work.

Her thesis, completed in 2016, examined how deep roots allow wheat to draw water from lower soil layers during drought. It included three years of field measurements on the Institute's terraces at Corran Heights, and a set of genetic markers for rooting depth that later became central to Seedvault North.

Founding Seedvault North

In the autumn of 2016 Imogen Fairbairn, the chair of the Verdant Seeds Cooperative, visited the Institute with records of fifteen years of harvests that showed a doubling of drought losses. She asked whether the Institute could help to breed a wheat variety better suited to the region's drier summers. Brandvik asked Raghunathan, who was then finishing her thesis, whether she would lead the program. Raghunathan says that she hesitated for a day, because she knew that the work would take at least a decade, and then agreed.

The project began in the spring of 2017. She recruited Olumide Hartigan, a plant breeder from a commercial seed company, to manage the breeding program, and Beatrix Nakamura, an agronomist, to run the field trials. In the first summer they traveled to more than two hundred farms and collected 340 traditional wheat lines, recording the farmers' own knowledge of each line. She regards this work as the most important of her career, because it brought the farmers into the project from the start.

The breeding program and Brindlewheat-3

Raghunathan's method combines conventional crossing with genetic analysis. The team sequenced the genomes of all 340 lines, tested them under controlled drought, and crossed the best ones over several generations. She introduced marker-assisted selection, which allows seedlings to be tested for desired genes within days, and the technique cut the time to develop a variety from about fifteen years to about eight.

The first release, in 2022, was Brindlewheat-3, a variety that produced an average of 21 percent more grain than the standard in dry years and matched it in wet years. Fairbairn described the day it was announced at the Cooperative's annual meeting as one of the proudest in the Cooperative's history. By the end of 2023, about a third of the region's wheat farmers had planted it.

The November 2023 seed bank loss

In November 2023 the duplicate seed bank held at the Dunmarrow depot of the Merrow Cold Chain project was damaged when the depot's refrigeration failed during an electricity brownout. About 31 percent of the breeding lines in the duplicate were no longer viable. Raghunathan was told at 06:00 the next morning, and she went at once to Dunmarrow to move the surviving packets to a working room.

She directed the recovery over the following year. Most of the lost lines also existed in the main collection at the Institute, and were regrown, but 14 were held only at Dunmarrow. Eleven of these were recovered from seed kept by partner farmers; three were lost permanently. The work cost 640,000 crowns and delayed the barley program by eight months.

She wrote the incident report herself, and in it she said that her mistake had been to assume that two copies of a seed bank in two places were enough. The second copy, she realized, had a hidden dependency on the same electricity grid and the same type of equipment as the first. She negotiated a revised agreement with the cold chain's director, Dr. Farouk Haidari, which divides the duplicate between two depots, and she has proposed that the Cooperative fund an independent third store at Corran Heights.

Teaching and mentoring

Raghunathan teaches a graduate course on plant breeding at the Institute, and she supervises four doctoral students. She holds a weekly seminar at which any member of the team, including students, presents a problem or a result, and where anyone can challenge anything she says. She says that the seminar is the main reason the project's work has remained honest.

She has also created a program in which about thirty farmers each year spend a week at the Institute working alongside the researchers. She says that the farmers teach her at least as much as she teaches them, since they know things about their land that no instrument can measure.

Relationships with other projects

Raghunathan is on friendly terms with Dr. Bjorn Halvorsen, who leads the Tidewrack Tidal Array and who also studied under Brandvik. The two have lunch each spring, a tradition they began in 2018. She collaborates with the Wetmoor Water Reuse project, whose director, Dr. Naledi Mokoena, supplies reclaimed water for a trial on the Corran Heights terraces comparing Brindlewheat-3 under river water and reclaimed water. The first results suggested that the water source made no difference to yield or grain quality.

Her project shares weather data with the Beacon Hill Flood Barrier, whose modelers use the long climate records from the Corran Heights terraces. And it relies on the Merrow Cold Chain for secure cold storage, a dependency that she now manages with more care.

Outlook

The next goal is a barley variety resistant to a fungal disease that is spreading in the wetter fields of the region, with trials planned for 2026. She is also studying whether the rooting traits of Brindlewheat-3 can be transferred to oats. Her broader ambition, she says, is to leave behind a program that will outlast her, with a seed bank that is truly secure and a team of breeders who understand both the genetics and the farmers they work for.

Personal life

Raghunathan lives on Corran Heights with her husband, a cellist who teaches at the regional conservatory, and their son. She keeps a small allotment where she grows heritage varieties of beans, and she says that she tests her own cooking on the seed bank's staff, who are polite about it. She sings in a choir and credits it with teaching her to hold a long note, a skill she says is essential for a plant breeder.


Annex: Career timeline and colleagues' recollections

Timeline. 2011: begins doctoral research at the Marrow Institute of Applied Science under Professor Ottoline Brandvik. 2016: completes her doctorate on rooting depth in wheat. Autumn 2016: the Verdant Seeds Cooperative asks the Institute for a drought-tolerant wheat variety. Spring 2017: founds Seedvault North. 2018: all 340 collected lines are sequenced. 2019: the first crosses are made. 2022: Brindlewheat-3 is released. 14 November 2023: a cold store failure destroys 31 percent of the duplicate breeding lines. Summer 2024: the lost lines are largely recovered. 2026: barley trials are planned.

What colleagues say. Olumide Hartigan, the deputy director who manages breeding, says that Raghunathan has the rare ability to hold a plan for eight years in her head and still notice that a single seedling in a tray looks different. Beatrix Nakamura, the field manager, recalls that Raghunathan insists on walking every plot once a week, in all weathers, and that she once spent an hour kneeling in the rain over a plot that she had a feeling about. "It turned out to be a mislabeled stake," Nakamura says, "but she was right that something was wrong."

Imogen Fairbairn, the chair of the Verdant Seeds Cooperative, says that Raghunathan does something that very few scientists do: she asks farmers what they think and then writes down what they say. Dr. Naledi Mokoena, who supplies reclaimed water for the project's irrigation trial, says that Raghunathan was the first researcher she had met who asked what the water contained, rather than how much there was. And Professor Brandvik has said that her best former student is one who taught her how to ask a farmer a question.

The weekly seminar. Held on Thursdays at 16:00 in a room overlooking the terraces, the seminar is open to all of the project's staff and students. Each week one person presents either a problem or a result. The rule is that the presenter may not be interrupted for the first ten minutes, and after that anyone may challenge anything. Raghunathan keeps a record of the seminar, which now runs to seven years of notes. She says that the notes show how often a problem was solved by someone who did not work on it.

The seed collection trips. During the first summer, in 2017, Raghunathan and her team visited more than two hundred farms, collecting seed and asking each farmer to describe what the variety was good for. A farmer in the northern valleys told her that his family's wheat had survived the drought of 1976, and that his grandmother had always kept the seed in a stone jar in the cellar. The wheat, catalogued as line 114, turned out to have the deepest roots of any of the 340 lines, and it is one of the parents of Brindlewheat-3.

The November 2023 loss in detail. Of the 412 breeding lines in the duplicate bank, 128 were no longer viable. For 14 lines that existed only at Dunmarrow, the team traced seed held by partner farmers. Three lines, designated BW-211, BW-217, and BW-230, could not be recovered. Raghunathan has said that she thinks about those three lines every week, and that she keeps a framed photograph of the field plot where BW-217 was last grown.

On resilience. Raghunathan wrote in her incident report that the lesson was that redundancy needs to be genuine. She asked the Institute to review all of its collections for hidden shared dependencies, a task assigned to Dr. Kasper Idowu, whose review found that two of the Institute's three other collections had similar weaknesses. She says that she is now more worried about the dependencies she cannot see than those she can.

Teaching. In addition to her graduate course on plant breeding, Raghunathan gives a public lecture each spring at the Corran Heights village hall. The lectures are attended by farmers, schoolchildren, and retirees, and she says that the questions from retirees are the best, because they remember when the weather was different.

Advice. She tells young researchers to spend one week a year working on a farm, because the farm will teach them what the data cannot. She also says that every breeding program should keep a record of its failures as carefully as its successes, since a line that was discarded this year may be exactly what is needed in thirty years.
