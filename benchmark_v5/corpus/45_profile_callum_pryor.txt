Profile: Callum Pryor

Callum Pryor is an offshore engineer and the director of the Ferrous Bay Offshore Wind farm, the largest renewable energy project in the Calder Reach. He has led the project since March 2019 and oversaw its construction, its commissioning in 2022, and its response to the cable fault and regional brownout of November 2023. He is known for a safety-first approach and for a belief that the project's responsibilities go beyond the megawatt-hours it sells.

Early life and education

Pryor was born in a port town on the east coast, the son of a trawler skipper and a harbor clerk. He went to sea at sixteen as a deckhand on his father's boat, and spent three years fishing before he decided that he wanted to understand the machinery rather than just operate it. He studied marine engineering at a maritime college, and qualified as a ship's engineer officer in 2005.

Career before Ferrous Bay

After a few years at sea on supply vessels serving oil platforms, Pryor moved ashore in 2010 to join an offshore wind developer as a marine coordinator. He progressed to become the site manager of an operating wind farm of 80 turbines, where he was responsible for the crew transfer vessels, the maintenance schedule, and the safety of about 150 technicians. He says that the job taught him the difference between a safety culture on paper and one in practice: on paper, everyone has the right to stop a job, but in practice the right is only real if the person who uses it is thanked.

At the wind farm he introduced a policy that any technician who stopped a job because they felt unsafe would receive a personal thank-you from the site manager the same day, even if the concern proved unfounded. He says that the number of near-miss reports tripled in the first six months, and that the number of lost-time injuries fell by half in two years.

Appointment to Ferrous Bay

When the Ferrous Bay project company was formed in 2018, with the Lumen Grid Company and a municipal pension fund as shareholders, it advertised for a director with offshore wind experience. Pryor applied and was appointed in March 2019. He moved to Dunmarrow with his family and set about recruiting a team. He chose Saoirse Valdez, a wind turbine engineer, as his deputy for commissioning and operations, and Gustav Nilsen, a former ship's officer, to head marine operations.

Building the wind farm

The project's construction took three years. Pryor negotiated the contracts for the turbines, foundations, and export cable, and the Harbourgate Mutual Bank's loan of 120 million crowns, which was the largest in the bank's history. He insisted that the rotor blades be made locally by Corran Mill, the polymer plant of the Brannock Foundry Group, which built a dedicated production line, and he visited the mill every month during production. He says that using a local blade manufacturer was one of the project's best decisions.

The installation took place between 2021 and 2022, working to a schedule that avoided the seabird breeding season. The first turbine produced electricity in May 2022 and the final one was commissioned in September. The wind farm reached its full capacity of 240 megawatts in October, and the official opening, in November, was attended by the chair of the Calder Reach Development Board, Hester Vandermeer.

The November 2023 storm

On 14 November 2023 a storm damaged the export cable at the shoreline and disconnected the entire wind farm from the grid at 15:08. Pryor was on board a crew transfer vessel in the bay at the time, observing the turbines' shutdown, and he saw the nacelles feather and the blades slow. He was told about the fault by radio within a minute. He has said that he knew at once that the loss of a third of the region's electricity in a single second would have consequences far beyond the wind farm.

The brownout that followed affected hospitals, factories, and storage depots, including the Isotope Works at the St. Idris Hospital Trust and the Merrow Cold Chain's depot at Dunmarrow. Pryor reported the event to the Development Board and the bank within three hours, and he published a full report on 12 January 2024, as promised. In it he acknowledged that the surf zone protection had been inadequate and that the annual survey had been carried out at the wrong state of the tide.

He wrote that the incident had changed his view of the project's responsibilities. He had thought of the wind farm as a producer of electricity whose risks were financial and operational. After November 2023, he said, he thought of it also as a part of the safety net of the region, and he committed to treating its reliability with the same seriousness as a hospital would treat its power supply.

Response and the second cable

Pryor made the case to the Development Board for a second export cable on a different route, with a landing site about nine kilometers north of the first. The Board agreed in principle in 2024 to fund it, at an estimated cost of 31 million crowns, and the bank said that it would support the new financing if the benefits for resilience were demonstrated. He says that the cable will be the most important investment the project makes.

Management style

Pryor is a quiet, observant man who prefers to speak last in a meeting. He spends about one day a week on the water and says that no dashboard can replace the sight of a turbine from a boat deck. He insists on a weekly "lessons learned" session, led by Valdez, in which any incident or near miss, however small, is examined and the notes are shared with the region's other offshore projects. Nilsen says that Pryor remembers the name of every technician on every crew transfer vessel, and asks after their families.

Relationships

Pryor works closely with the Lumen Grid Company, which buys the farm's electricity, and its chief executive, Anselm Rask. He also works with the Dunmarrow Port Automation project, whose quayside was used for the assembly of turbines and where his operations base is leased. He is on friendly terms with Cormac Adebayo, who leads the port project, and with Dr. Bjorn Halvorsen of Tidewrack, with whom he shares notes on marine operations. He consults the Marrow Institute of Applied Science on environmental monitoring, and he has a good relationship with the fishing associations, partly because the project employs several former fishermen as vessel crew.

Personal life

Pryor lives in Dunmarrow with his wife, a physiotherapist, and their three children. He is a keen sailor and owns a small wooden boat that his father built. He volunteers as a crew member on the Dunmarrow lifeboat, a commitment that he says reminds him what it means to depend on equipment that must work.

Views on the future

Pryor believes that the next decade of offshore wind in the Calder Reach will be defined by resilience rather than scale. He hopes to build a second phase of 30 turbines further offshore, but only once the second export cable is in place. He also wants to explore how the wind farm's output could be paired with storage, such as the Quarrel Hill plant, to reduce the effect of a sudden loss of generation. He says that the project's job is not just to produce power but to do so in a way that does not surprise the people who depend on it.


Annex: Career timeline and colleagues' recollections

Timeline. 2005: qualifies as a ship's engineer officer. 2005 to 2010: serves on offshore supply vessels. 2010: joins an offshore wind developer as a marine coordinator. 2014 to 2019: site manager of an operating wind farm of 80 turbines. March 2019: appointed director of the Ferrous Bay Offshore Wind farm. July 2019: the loan of 120 million crowns is signed. October 2020: the first monopile is driven. May 2022: the first turbine produces power. October 2022: the farm reaches 240 megawatts. 14 November 2023: the export cable fails during a storm. 28 December 2023: the farm returns to service. 2024: the shoreline is rebuilt and the second cable is approved in principle.

What colleagues say. Saoirse Valdez, the deputy director, says that Pryor taught her the most important sentence in offshore work: "I am not sure." She recalls that during the first winter of operation he stopped a maintenance trip because the weather forecast had changed by half a meter of wave height, and that the other crew felt a flash of irritation, which turned to relief when the sea became rough an hour later. "He was right," she says, "and he thanked the crew for turning round."

Gustav Nilsen, the head of marine operations, says that Pryor sits on the bridge of the crew transfer vessel on the first trip of each season, not to direct but to watch. "He learns from the faces," Nilsen says. Anselm Rask of the Lumen Grid Company describes him as the most honest generator he deals with, and recalls that Pryor telephoned him within three minutes of the cable fault, before the control center's alarms had finished.

The safety rule. The safety rule that Pryor introduced at Ferrous Bay states that any person may stop any job at any time if they feel it is unsafe, and that the person who does so will receive a personal thank-you from the director within 24 hours. In the first two years, 63 jobs were stopped under the rule. In 58 cases the concern turned out to be justified. Pryor sent a handwritten note in every case, including the five that were not.

The surf zone survey. The investigation of the November 2023 cable fault found that the annual survey of the surf zone had been carried out at high tide, when the rock armor was under water and the gap could not be seen. Pryor has said that this was the mistake he regrets most, because he had approved the survey plan and had not asked at what state of the tide it would be carried out. He now requires every survey plan to state the tide, the weather, and the method, and he signs them personally.

The repair vessel. The cable ship that carried out the repair in December 2023 had to wait two weeks for a window of calm weather. Pryor spent the Christmas period at Dunmarrow, and visited the vessel's crew with a box of food from his wife. He says that the sight of twelve jointers working in a tent on a rolling deck, on Christmas Eve, was the moment when he understood what it meant to run a piece of infrastructure.

The community fund. The community benefit payment of the project is 0.4 million crowns a year, distributed among the western coastal towns. Pryor insists that the decision on how to spend it be taken by a panel of residents, not by the project. In 2023 the panel funded a lifeboat station extension, a swimming pool refurbishment, and four scholarships. Pryor, who volunteers on the Dunmarrow lifeboat, abstained from the vote on the lifeboat station.

Relationships with other directors. Pryor is on friendly terms with Cormac Adebayo at Dunmarrow Port, whose quayside he uses, and with Dr. Bjorn Halvorsen of Tidewrack, with whom he shares notes on marine operations. He calls Teodora Valkanova of Quarrel Hill "the person who keeps the lights on when I turn them off." After the brownout he visited Dr. Tobias Ndlovu of Isotope Works and Dr. Farouk Haidari of Merrow Cold Chain, to apologize in person for the consequences of the cable fault for their facilities.

Advice. He tells new technicians that the sea will take any shortcut they give it, and that every procedure was written by someone who learned the hard way. He also says that the best offshore engineers are the ones who are a little afraid.
