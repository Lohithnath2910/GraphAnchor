Organization Profile: The Verdant Seeds Cooperative

The Verdant Seeds Cooperative is an agricultural cooperative owned by about 1,400 farming families in the Calder Reach. It was founded in 1921 by a group of hill farmers who wanted to buy seed and fertilizer together and sell their grain at better prices. Its headquarters is at Corran Heights, in a stone building beside the Marrow Institute of Applied Science's field station, and it employs about 230 people. Imogen Fairbairn has chaired the Cooperative since 2015.

What the cooperative does

The Cooperative provides its members with seed, fertilizer, and agricultural machinery, buys their grain and oilseed crops, and markets them on their behalf. It owns a seed processing plant at Corran Heights, two grain stores, a feed mill, and a fleet of grain trucks. It also provides agronomic advice, a farm accounting service, and a savings and loan scheme. Its annual turnover is about 96 million crowns.

Members elect a council of fifteen, which oversees the management, and one vote is allocated to each member regardless of the size of the farm. Surpluses are returned to members in proportion to their trade with the Cooperative. Fairbairn, who farms 140 hectares of wheat and barley in the hills above Corran Heights, was elected chair in 2015 on a platform of investing in plant breeding.

Seed breeding and Seedvault North

The Cooperative's most significant strategic decision in recent years was to commission a new wheat variety from the Marrow Institute of Applied Science. In 2016 Fairbairn presented the Institute with fifteen years of harvest records, which showed that the number of seasons with serious drought losses had doubled. This led to the creation of the Seedvault North project in 2017, led by Dr. Priya Raghunathan, and in 2022 to the release of a drought-tolerant wheat variety, Brindlewheat-3.

The Cooperative multiplies and distributes the seed of Brindlewheat-3. Its seed processing plant at Corran Heights cleans, treats, and packs the seed, and the Cooperative's members buy it at a price that includes a royalty. The royalty funds a large share of the Seedvault North project's budget, which is why the Cooperative describes itself as the project's principal funder, even though the project is hosted and partly paid for by the Institute. About a third of the region's wheat farmers had adopted the variety within two years of its release.

The agreement between the Cooperative and the Institute sets out how the revenue from seed sales is divided and how the project's decisions are taken. Fairbairn sits on the project's steering group, and the Cooperative's technical committee, a group of eight farmers, reviews the results of each season's trials.

Use of reclaimed water

About 1,800 hectares of the farmland of the Cooperative's members is irrigated with reclaimed water from the Wetmoor Water Reuse project, which supplies the water to farms through a dedicated pipeline network. The Cooperative negotiated the price with the project and organized the connection of its members' farms. Fairbairn says that reclaimed water has made the members' yields much more stable in dry summers, and that it has been one of the most important changes in regional farming in her lifetime.

Storage and the cold chain

The Cooperative stores some of its seed and produce in the refrigerated depots of the Merrow Cold Chain project, especially the depot at Dunmarrow, from which export consignments are shipped. In November 2023, a failure of the refrigeration at the Dunmarrow depot during the electricity brownout affected some of the Cooperative's stored seed and produce. The Cooperative lost goods worth about 0.3 million crowns, and a part of the duplicate seed bank of Seedvault North, which the Cooperative had helped to establish, was also damaged. Fairbairn wrote to the depot's director to ask for a written commitment to improved safeguards, and the Cooperative contributed 120,000 crowns toward the cost of rebuilding the seed bank.

Governance and member relations

The Cooperative's council meets monthly. Each spring, it holds an annual general meeting in the Corran Heights village hall, attended by several hundred members. The Cooperative's principal committees cover technical matters, finance, and marketing. The technical committee, whose members are practicing farmers, has the right to review and comment on all of the Cooperative's investments in research.

The Cooperative also operates a young farmers' program, which provides training and a small grant to farmers under 35, and a program of farm visits in which members visit each other's holdings to share practice. Fairbairn has said that the Cooperative's greatest asset is the trust among members, and that every decision it takes should be tested against whether it would preserve that trust.

Finances

The Cooperative's balance sheet is conservative. It holds about 28 million crowns in reserves, and its borrowing is limited. It banks with the Harbourgate Mutual Bank, and many of its members have personal accounts there. Its surplus in 2023 was 4.1 million crowns, of which 2.9 million was returned to members as a rebate on purchases, and 1.2 million was retained for investment.

Challenges

The Cooperative's main challenge is climate. The region's summers are drier, and its members' farms are small and often on marginal land. The Cooperative has responded with seed breeding, with the use of reclaimed water, and with an advisory program on soil management. A second challenge is the aging of the membership: the average age of members is 57, and many farms lack a successor. The young farmers' program is the Cooperative's response, but Fairbairn acknowledges that it is not enough.

Collaboration with the Institute

The Cooperative works with the Marrow Institute on research beyond Seedvault North. It hosts trial plots for the Institute's soil carbon study, it provides weather data, and it takes part in the Institute's annual open day. The Institute's director, Professor Ottoline Brandvik, is an honorary member of the Cooperative, an honor given to her in 2019.

The Cooperative in the regional economy

The Cooperative is one of the region's largest employers outside the major cities, and its purchasing decisions shape the economy of the hill districts. It buys about 30 percent of its equipment from the Brannock Foundry Group's customers and sources its trucks locally. It exports about a quarter of its members' grain through the Dunmarrow Port, using the services of Strand & Keel Shipping to carry it to the quay.

Environmental stewardship

The Cooperative encourages members to adopt sustainable practices. It runs a program to restore hedgerows and field margins, which has planted 62 kilometers of hedges since 2018, and it supports no-till farming on suitable soils. In 2023 it began measuring the carbon stored in its members' soils, with the help of the Institute, and plans to offer members a payment for carbon sequestration.

Outlook

Fairbairn's priorities for the next five years include the barley program at Seedvault North, a new seed store at Corran Heights to provide an independent third location for the duplicate seed bank, and the extension of the reclaimed water network to further farms. She also wants the Cooperative to be more open to young and new members, and has proposed a reduced membership fee for farmers under 30.


Annex: The Cooperative's year, assets, and people

The farming year

The Cooperative's year is organized around the cereal cycle. Autumn is the season of sowing: members collect winter wheat seed, including Brindlewheat-3, from the Corran Heights processing plant in September and October, and the Cooperative's advisers visit farms to review sowing plans. Winter is quieter, and is used for training, the annual general meeting in February, and the planning of next season's trials with Seedvault North. Spring brings the fertilizer season and the first field visits. Summer is harvest, and the Cooperative's grain stores at Corran Heights and Mossgate receive the crop from late July.

Assets and infrastructure

The seed processing plant at Corran Heights was built in 1987 and rebuilt in 2019 to handle the Brindlewheat-3 multiplication. It has a capacity of about 6,000 tonnes a year. The two grain stores have a combined capacity of 38,000 tonnes, and the feed mill produces about 12,000 tonnes a year of livestock feed. The Cooperative's grain trucks, 26 in total, deliver to the Dunmarrow Port, where the grain is loaded into containers or bulk ships. The Cooperative also owns a small laboratory that tests seed viability and grain quality.

Key staff

The Cooperative's general manager is Percival Atherton, who runs the day-to-day operation and reports to Fairbairn and the council. The head of the seed division is Dr. Rosalind Maudsley, who coordinates the multiplication of Brindlewheat-3 with Seedvault North's breeding manager, Olumide Hartigan. The head of the finance division is Gwendolyn Thornbury, and the chief agronomist is Vusumuzi Collings, who leads the team of twelve advisers. The council's vice chair is Oluwaseun Brightwell, a farmer from the northern valleys.

Relations with Seedvault North

The revenue-sharing agreement with the Institute gives the Cooperative 40 percent of the net income from Brindlewheat-3 seed sales, the Institute 35 percent, and the Seedvault North project 25 percent for reinvestment. In 2023 the seed sales generated about 1.9 million crowns net. The Cooperative uses its share to fund the multiplication plant's operating costs and a program of subsidized seed for young farmers.

The agreement also sets up a joint committee that reviews the project's research priorities each year. In 2023 the committee decided to give priority to barley resistant to a fungal disease, and to the recovery of the lines lost at the Dunmarrow depot. The Cooperative contributed 120,000 crowns to the recovery work, and members volunteered to grow lines on their farms.

The reclaimed water scheme

The Cooperative's agreement with the Wetmoor Water Reuse project, signed in 2020, commits the Cooperative to buy a minimum annual volume of reclaimed water at a fixed price for ten years. The water is delivered through the purple pipeline network to 74 member farms. In the dry summer of 2022 the farms using reclaimed water had yields 14 percent higher than comparable farms using river water, and the Cooperative's advisers have worked with Mokoena's staff to adapt irrigation schedules.

During the project's shutdown in April 2022, when the Wetmoor plant was out of service for twelve days, the Cooperative's farmers were able to switch to river water because it was early in the season and demand was low. Fairbairn said that the episode had been a reminder that the Cooperative should not depend wholly on one source.

Cold storage and the Dunmarrow incident

The Cooperative stores about 1,100 tonnes of seed and produce in the Dunmarrow depot of the Merrow Cold Chain, mostly seed of high-value crops and export produce. In the November 2023 incident, goods worth about 0.3 million crowns were lost. The Cooperative's quality manager, Eleonora Vance, led the Cooperative's response, which included an inspection of every affected consignment and the preparation of claims.

The Cooperative has since asked Merrow to provide it with a direct alert for temperature excursions, a service that was introduced in the spring of 2024. It has also begun to store a part of its stock in the Mossgate depot, to reduce its dependence on a single site.

Young farmers' program

The young farmers' program, started in 2018, has trained 112 farmers under 35. Each participant receives a mentor from the Cooperative's council, a business plan review, and a grant of up to 8,000 crowns toward equipment. About 70 percent of participants are still farming five years after joining, a rate that the Cooperative regards as a success in view of the difficulty of entering farming.

Community and heritage

The Cooperative maintains a museum of farming at Corran Heights, in a restored barn, which holds a collection of historic tools and seed packets from the 1920s. The annual harvest festival, held in September, draws about 4,000 visitors and includes a ploughing competition, a seed swap, and a cooking contest in which cooks must use a variety of heritage wheat. Fairbairn judges the cooking contest each year and says that it is the hardest task of her chairmanship.
